Dollar General: A Tale of Two Prices

The FTC sued over the two prices. ATCSTW followed the rest of the machine. It was the best of prices, it was the worst of prices.

Dollar General is at the center of an FTC lawsuit built on 29,265 "Did Not Honor Marked Price" complaints — and ATCSTW ran the Labor Department's own records and counted 883 inspections and $25,012,602 in initial OSHA penalties behind it. This is not a story about a few wrong price tags. It is the story of a company that takes from the same working-class town three ways — at the register, at the time clock, and at the map, where in town after town it is often the only door left — and sends the money up. On October 26, 2026, the federal government sued over the first one. The lawsuit is the door. The house is bigger. That's our argument; the receipts for each leg follow, labeled as they come.

The eggs

Start in the aisle, not the courthouse. A shopper's eggs were priced $5.10 on the shelf and $6.35 at the register — same store, same eggs, Ohio, 2023. His complaint is quoted in the federal filing — the government alleges the shelf price was $5.10 and the register charged $6.35.

Two prices. One store. Only one of them is the price you were promised.

Now multiply the question — not the money, the question — by 21,148 stores, Dollar General's own count as of July 31, 2026.

Monday: the door opens

On October 26, 2026, the FTC and 20 states sued Dollar General in federal court in the Middle District of Tennessee — 29 counts — alleging seven years of shelf prices that did not survive the register, from January 1, 2019 to March 31, 2026. The government alleges that is the case Dollar General must now answer.

Dollar General's answer, fairly: it has cooperated since February, calls the government's settlement demand "orders of magnitude larger" than any realistic harm, in the company's own words, and says it will defend itself. The non-public investigation began with a federal demand dated January 30, 2026; Dollar General handed over more than 200,000 documents — by Dollar General's own Form 8-K account.

The government finally walked in. What they found is only the first room.

Start where the shopper starts. At the shelf.

The register: the mechanism

The government alleges the machine works like this. Register prices update centrally and automatically. Shelf tags change by hand. The company gives that job five hours a week — "Compliance Tuesday" — and the price changes are, in the complaint's words, "generally increases," so the lag usually runs one direction — toward an overcharge, in the complaint's words.

The government alleges 29,265 customers filed "Did Not Honor Marked Price" complaints with Dollar General from 2023 to 2026. The government alleges FTC undercover shoppers were net-overcharged at all four stores they visited — in Cleveland, 13 of 58 prices were inaccurate.

The shelf is the promise. The register is the truth they charge you.

The ledger of knocking

And the government had been knocking about this for years before Monday.

The government alleges, from state inspection records pleaded in the complaint: one Kansas store failed 44 pricing inspections. A Nephi, Utah store failed 10 in a row — 15 of 19 inspections in all. Iowa: eight pricing inspections at seven stores — failed every one. Wisconsin: 9% of scanned products overcharged, averaging 17% above the shelf price.

The customer underneath it all: households earning $35,000 or less are about 60% of Dollar General's revenue — the government's filing, from the company's own descriptions. The CEO has said it plainly on earnings calls: the core customer "runs out of money before the month runs out."

They didn't get caught once. They got caught as a routine — and paid the fines like a utility bill.

That's the shopper. Now clock in — because in these towns, she's the same person.

The time clock

The record shows OSHA placed Dollar General in the Severe Violator Enforcement Program in October 2022 — blocked exits, blocked extinguishers, blocked electrical panels, unsafe stacking.

ATCSTW ran it: ATCSTW ran the Labor Department's own bulk data and counted 883 inspections, 1,130 violations, and $25,012,602 in initial penalties. $19,706,687.59 still stands as current penalties.

The record shows that in July 2024 the company signed a $12 million corporate-wide OSHA settlement with $100,000-a-day teeth, capped at $500,000.

The EEOC record shows: $6 million in 2019 over race and background checks, and $1 million in 2023 over disability screening — 498 applicants turned away.

The honest small print, at full size: ATCSTW counted the Wage & Hour record at 137 cases and $64,018 in back wages. Our read: a model that keeps every individual claim small and local produces a small federal total.

Our argument in one line: the understaffing that leaves the shelf tag stale is the understaffing that leaves the fire exit blocked.

She clocks out, she shops, and then the town itself pays the third time.

The territory

The government's filing, drawing on the company's own descriptions, puts the map plainly: roughly 80% of stores sit in towns of 20,000 or fewer, and about 75% of Americans live within five miles of one. In town after town it is often the only store left — the complaint's own framing, from the company's descriptions of its footprint.

The complaint's own exhibits show shoppers who caught the overcharge and paid anyway — "she did not want to argue further, she paid and left."

The benefit card: Dollar General does not disclose what share of its sales is SNAP. Say the hole out loud — it is not company-disclosed. The estimates below live in this article only, each with its own denominator, and they are never blended.

Three ways in. Now follow the money out.

The pipe

Dollar General's own annual report lists its margin levers, in its words: "pricing and markdown optimization."

In its Q2 FY2026 results — the quarter ended July 31, 2026, after the complaint's window closed on March 31, 2026 — Dollar General reported gross margin expanded 127 basis points to 32.6%. The scale, labeled as ATCSTW arithmetic: 100 basis points of margin on $42.7 billion of sales (FY2025 net sales, per its Form 10-K) is about $427 million a year. No filing ties that margin to the price failures, and we don't claim it does.

The CEO's pay against the median worker's, from the company's own proxy filed April 7, 2026: 432 to 1 — $8,163,197 against $18,876.

Political footprint, in one breath: the record shows no company PAC exists — ATCSTW certified the negative in the FEC registry — and ATCSTW ran FEC Schedule A and counted, across four decades, Dollar General employees' total federal giving at $454,373 (1986–2026). The CEO Todd Vasos's own giving: $9,500 — every dollar to RILA PAC, the retail industry leaders' PAC. And ATCSTW ran the federal lobbying filings: $207,500 across four filings in 2025, through one firm, the Vogel Group — in filings under the client name "Dollar General", the exact-name LDA form ATCSTW searched.

Footnote on the giving, on the closed six-bucket basis: REP $170,543 · DEM $29,396 · UNCLASSIFIED $120,543 · UNRESOLVED $126,976 · LIB $5,916 · IND $999 = $454,373. The unassigned majority must be stated with it: 54.5% of that money carries no party designation at all, and 71.9% of 2024 giving is unclassified in the FEC's own master file.

The government connection, said as our argument: the federal complaint never mentions shareholders, investors, or earnings — not once in 95 pages. The weld is ours: "dynamic pricing by neglect" — an algorithm asks what the traffic will bear; Dollar General built traffic that cannot leave, then let the register run ahead of the paper tag for seven years. That is a functional argument, not a claim that Dollar General runs pricing algorithms or electronic shelf labels. The paper tags are the point.

Uber needs an algorithm to find your breaking point. Dollar General just bought the only store in town.

So what happens now — because one state already showed what accountability looks like.

The reckoning, the defense, and the close

Two weeks before the federal filing, the record shows New York settled its case: $1,875,000 in donated food and goods, at retail value, to Foodlink, the regional food bank, and an independent auditor walking every New York store twice a year, with 24 hours to fix any wrong price.

The federal case now asks for injunctions, money, and per-violation penalties that run to five figures per violation in at least two states — across seven years and 29,265 complaints, nobody outside that courtroom knows the total, and the government has not published one. Neither will we.

The defense, one fair breath, in their words: "Nothing could be further from the truth… our goal is 100% accuracy." They cooperated for eight months; they settled in New York without admitting anything. Say it straight — then the register, the time clock, and the territory answer it.

A Dollar General town has one store, one employer, one register. The government sued over the shelf tags. The shelf tags are the smallest of the three.
